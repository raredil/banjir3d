# Methods of the Banjir3D flood engine (version 2)

This text describes only what the running system does. Every number the engine uses is either read from a named dataset or fixed by a cited method; the user sets only external forcing (rain, sea level and simulated time).

## 1. Model domain and grid

The model grid is the FABDEM V1-2 bare-earth terrain [@hawker2022] reprojected to WGS 84 / UTM zone 48S at 30 m, covering mainland DKI Jakarta plus a 10 km buffer (about 52 × 51 km, 2.98 million cells). Cells outside the buffer polygon are inactive, and a run can also use a user-drawn box or blocks of 2 × 2 or 3 × 3 cells (60 m or 90 m) for faster runs.

A drawn box can also be shown on cells of 10, 5, 2 or 1 m. No open bare-earth terrain is finer than 30 m here, so the flow is solved on the same 30 m grid as a 30 m run of the box, the grid checked against recorded floods (section 8), and the fine cells only draw its result: the water surface of the 30 m cells, interpolated bilinearly, minus the land surface interpolated bilinearly to the fine cell. Area, depth, buildings and people affected are therefore identical at 30 m and on every finer cell size; only the drawing and the exported rasters are finer. Until November 2026 building footprints were cut out of the drawn water and a trial option solved the flow among the buildings on the fine cells (building blocks, @schubert2012; building porosity on a dual grid, @sanders2008 and @sanders2019). The cut-outs made the map look fragmented without changing a number, and the trial flow put the flooding in other places than the 30 m run (critical success index 0.06–0.17 against 30 m, `verification/fine-consistency/`) because below 30 m the ground is only interpolated and the buildings take over from the built-area roughness on which the checks rest, so both were removed from the app (ADR 0019; the code stays for the verification scripts).

Cells of 60 and 90 m average the 30 m terrain over blocks of 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 cells; a block that holds a channel keeps the channel's level. Averaging smooths hollows, river banks and raised roads, so results differ somewhat between 30, 60 and 90 m, as in any grid model; 30 m is the reference, and 90 m is used for the whole-region checks of section 8 because it needs up to about 27 times less computing (nine times fewer cells, three times longer time steps).

## 2. Hydrodynamics

The engine solves the two-dimensional shallow water equations in the local inertial form of @bates2010, the scheme used by LISFLOOD-FP and, without advection, by SFINCS [@leijnse2021; @vanormondt2025]. The momentum equation for the unit-width discharge q in each direction is the following.

    ∂q/∂t + g h ∂η/∂x + g n² q |q| / h^(7/3) = 0

Here h is depth, η = z + h the water level, g gravity and n Manning's coefficient; friction is treated semi-implicitly and the face depth is the difference between the higher water level and the higher bed of the two cells, as in @bates2010. The local inertial form is accurate for the low-Froude flow typical of urban floodplains and loses accuracy near critical flow [@dealmeida2013], so face discharge is capped at the critical value h√(gh).

The time step follows the Courant condition Δt = α Δx / √(g h_max) with α = 0.7, the value used by LISFLOOD-FP, and an upper limit of 15 s. Until November 2026 the engine used α = 0.5; on a 4.4 km box at 30 m and on the whole region at 90 m the two give the same maximum depths (root-mean-square difference 1–6 mm, critical success index 0.999 for cells deeper than 0.15 m) and α = 0.7 runs about 30 % faster (`verification/speed/`). Mass is conserved by updating depth from the net face fluxes, and a limiter scales outgoing fluxes so that no cell gives away more water than it holds. A face carries flow only when its depth exceeds 0.001 m, the default threshold of LISFLOOD-FP, so thin runoff sheets still drain.

## 3. Terrain conditioning with mapped rivers

OpenStreetMap river and canal polygons and river, canal and stream centre lines (snapshot of October 2026) are rasterised onto the grid. Each channel cell takes the lowest terrain value within one cell, which is the water surface the DEM recorded, and along each centre line a one-dimensional morphological opening over five cells (150 m) removes humps narrower than that window, such as bridges, culverts and road embankments that a DEM keeps as dams. No depth is added below the recorded water surface, because no open dataset gives channel bathymetry; the channel volume below that surface is taken as already filled by base flow at the start of an event.

Primary and secondary drains of the Dinas Sumber Daya Air of DKI Jakarta (Jakarta Satu geoportal) [@dsda2026], which record width and depth for each channel, are added as channel cells. Where a depth is given, the bed is lowered to the bank level (the median of the surrounding 3 × 3 terrain) minus the depth scaled by the share of the cell the channel occupies (depth × width / 30 m, width at most 30 m), unless the terrain is already lower. The carved part below the water surface the DEM recorded starts filled with water, as for the OpenStreetMap channels, so the drains add conveyance rather than empty storage.

The same agency maps 263 pump stations with their capacity (m³/s) and 65 polder systems. Each pump serves the polder it stands in, or the nearest polder within 500 m, since stations sit on the polder edge. The summed capacity of a polder is spread evenly over its land cells (not channels or sea) as a sink rate, so a fully wet polder loses exactly its pump capacity and a partly wet one loses less: the pumps are a lower bound. Pumped water leaves the model. The app can switch the pumps off as a scenario.

Land subsidence moves the terrain after it was measured. The FABDEM terrain comes from Copernicus DEM radar acquisitions of 2011–2015, taken here as the epoch 2013.0. Subsidence rates come from the Sinking City dataset [@shabrina2026]: annual Sentinel-1 line-of-sight velocities for 2016–2024 from LiCSBAS [@morishita2020] on LiCSAR interferograms of ascending frame 098A_09673_121312 [@lazecky2020], kriged to 30 m. Single years are noisy, so each cell takes the mean of the nine annual velocities, divided by the up component of the line-of-sight vector (0.77–0.79 over Jakarta, from the LiCSAR frame metadata) to give vertical motion, assuming horizontal motion is negligible; upward values are set to zero, as the dataset authors do in their maps. Before each run the terrain of every cell is lowered by its rate times the years between 2013.0 and the simulated date (the event date for an observed flood, today for a scenario). The map covers DKI Jakarta only; elsewhere the rate is zero.

DEMNAS, the 8 m national DEM of Badan Informasi Geospasial, is shown on the map but is not the model terrain. Averaged to the 30 m grid, it lies a median 0.79 m above FABDEM in built-up cells, and regressed on the excess of the Copernicus surface model over FABDEM it keeps a slope of 0.62, that is about 60 % of building and tree height; water is filled at a constant level near −3.3 m.

## 4. Land cover, roughness and infiltration

Land cover is the Esri / Impact Observatory 10 m annual map of the newest available year, 2024 at build time. Each 30 m cell receives the area-weighted Manning n of its nine 10 m pixels, using HEC-RAS land-classification values after @chow1959 [@hecras2d]; built-up land uses n = 0.15, an effective roughness that represents buildings removed from the bare-earth DEM, an approach @beretta2018 found about as accurate as explicit building geometry. Mapped channel cells (section 3) take the open-water value n = 0.035 instead, the upper end of the range for clean natural and excavated channels in @chow1959: a river is often narrower than the 10 m land-cover pixels, so its own cell would otherwise carry the roughness of the buildings along its banks. Until November 2026 they did, which made every channel about four times too rough; rivers then spilled over their banks on 30 m cells but stayed in them on 10 m cells, so results changed with the cell size (`verification/fine-consistency/`). A coarse cell that holds a channel keeps the channel's level and roughness.

Rain becomes runoff through the NRCS curve number method [@nrcs1986]. For cumulative rain P and retention S = 25400/CN − 254 (mm), direct runoff is Q = (P − 0.2S)² / (P + 0.8S) for P > 0.2S and zero otherwise; each step adds the increase of Q to the cell, so the rest of the rain infiltrates. Curve numbers come from TR-55 tables for each land-cover class and hydrologic soil group, built-up pixels count as impervious (CN 98) and a cell takes the area-weighted value of its pixels, which is the TR-55 composite curve number for mixed cells.

The hydrologic soil group is derived from SoilGrids 2.0 sand and clay contents averaged over 0–100 cm [@poggio2021], using the texture criteria of the NRCS soil-group chapter [@nrcs2009] that also underlie the global HYSOGs250m map [@ross2018]. In the Jakarta region the result is 83 % group D and 17 % group C, consistent with the clay-rich soils of the area. Antecedent moisture is the average condition (AMC II) of the method, the condition used in all checks of section 8. The app can switch to the dry (AMC I) or wet (AMC III) condition, chosen by the rain of the five days before the storm (growing-season limits of 35.6 and 53.3 mm), with the curve numbers converted as CN(I) = 4.2 CN(II) / (10 − 0.058 CN(II)) and CN(III) = 23 CN(II) / (10 + 0.13 CN(II)) [@chow1988]; the conversion applies to the model cells and to the upstream catchments, while the catchment lag keeps the AMC II value. This is how rain of earlier days or weeks enters a run: a weekly or monthly total spread evenly over a simulation would be a drizzle that floods nothing and would ignore the storm days inside it. Infiltration can also be switched off (every drop runs off) as a what-if for sealed green space. In the model area 85 % of the land is built-up in the 2024 map and the mean curve number of the land cells is 97, so infiltration matters most in the upstream catchments.

## 5. Inflow from upstream catchments

Flow directions are computed on the Copernicus GLO-90 DEM over 106.35–107.35° E and 5.95–7.00° S, which holds every catchment draining into the region. Mapped rivers and streams of the whole area (Overture Maps base theme, from OpenStreetMap) are lowered by 20 m before routing so that the D8 directions follow the mapped network on flat land, and canals are left out because they cross catchment divides. For every stream cell draining at least 1 km² the engine stores upstream area, area-mean curve number, mean slope and longest flow length.

When a run starts, the engine finds every stream that crosses into the run area and computes its inflow hydrograph from the same rain applied over its catchment. Runoff depth follows the curve number method, the lag time follows the NRCS watershed lag equation [@nrcs2010] and the discharge follows the NRCS dimensionless unit hydrograph [@nrcs2007], integrated over 5-minute steps so that the runoff volume is kept exactly. Catchment area that lies inside the run area, or that was already counted at an upstream entry, is removed so that no rain is counted twice.

## 6. Rain and sea level

The design-storm option takes a rain total P and a duration D of 1 to 72 h. Within the storm, the depth of the most intense t hours follows the Mononobe intensity–duration relation used in Indonesian practice [@maitsa2021], P (t/D)^(1/3), and the hourly increments are ordered with the alternating block method [@chow1988], so the whole total falls with its peak in the middle; for D = 24 h this is the Mononobe distribution of a 24-hour total. Durations above 24 h extend the relation beyond the day for which it was derived, which is an assumption. Until November 2026 the option took a 24-hour total R24 and kept only the depth of its most intense D hours (94.5 mm of a 150 mm R24 for D = 6 h), which users read as the total. For scale, Halim Perdanakusuma recorded 377 mm on 1 January 2020 [@lubis2022]; users can also type hourly depths. The observed-flood option replays the three floods of section 8 with the hourly GPM IMERG rain of every 0.1° cell [@huffman2023]: each model cell and each upstream catchment takes the rain of its nearest cell centre, as in the validation runs.

Sea cells, defined as water pixels at or below 0.5 m connected to the open sea, are held at the chosen sea level throughout a run. Water reaching any other edge of the run area leaves through a transmissive boundary that passes the discharge arriving at the adjacent face, capped at critical flow.

## 7. Display and export

The map shows depth per pixel by interpolating the water level bilinearly from the surrounding wet cells and subtracting the ground at that pixel, the water-level downscaling used for SFINCS results [@leijnse2021]. The ground used for drawing is the land surface: a river, canal or drain cell takes the mean level of the land around it, and this surface is interpolated bilinearly on every grid. Water inside a channel up to its banks is therefore the river itself and is not drawn as flood, water above the banks is drawn as one smooth sheet, and rivers do not show as a chain of 30 m squares; flood hazard maps usually mask permanent water the same way. This gives smooth water edges that follow the ground without inventing water where all surrounding cells are dry, and colours follow the depth classes 0.05, 0.15, 0.5, 1 and 2 m. Depth exports are GeoTIFF files in EPSG:32748, with the model depth per cell at 30–90 m and the drawn depth per fine cell at 10–1 m, together with a JSON record of all inputs, data versions, inflows and the water balance.

## 8. Verification

The WebGPU solver gives the same maximum depths as the CPU reference on real terrain to within 0.003 m, and both close the water balance to within 10⁻⁷ of the inflow. The scheme reproduces the analytical flood wave of @hunter2005 within 3 % of the inflow depth and matched SFINCS 2.4.0 on the same grid, rain and roughness with an RMSE of 0.06 m in maximum depth. These tests check the numerics and say nothing about agreement with real floods.

The engine was then run on the whole region at 90 m for three observed floods: 1 January 2020 for calibration, and 20 February 2021 and 4 March 2025 for validation. Rain came from the half-hourly GPM IMERG Final estimates on their 0.1° cells [@huffman2023], each upstream catchment receiving the rain of its own cell; the set-up is the app default (Jakarta Satu drains carved, terrain shifted by subsidence to the event date, channel roughness 0.035, average antecedent moisture, no pumps, no drain sink).

A citizen flood report from PetaBencana.id counts as hit when the model reaches 0.10 m on land, either in the cell holding the report or in the deepest land cell of the 3 × 3 window around it (about 270 m, since report locations are approximate). River channels and sea cells are excluded: a channel always holds water, and until November 2026 the score included them, so every report beside a river counted as hit. A score only means something next to what chance gives, so each rule is also applied to every land cell of DKI Jakarta: the share that passes is the hit rate of points placed at random. With the window rule the model hit 50 % of 260 reports in 2020, 47 % of 384 in 2021 and 41 % of 49 in 2025, against 43, 28 and 43 % for random points; with the same-cell rule 23, 21 and 14 % against 19, 11 and 19 %. The model is therefore clearly better than chance in 2021, slightly better in 2020 and not better in 2025 (few reports). The old rule that counted channel water gave 72, 75 and 74 % for the same runs (`verification/observed/scores-*-imerg-v6.json`). At the reports the modelled maximum depth on land has a median of 0.10, 0.10 and 0.07 m, where citizens reported 0.70, 0.65 and 0.55 m. In 2020 the model flooded 83 km² of DKI land deeper than 0.15 m, against the 156 km² and 390 RW the city government reported. Place by place, over 2,610 RW polygons of the Sinking City dataset [@shabrina2026] (an RW counts as observed flooded when it holds a citizen report, and as modelled flooded when at least 10 % of its land is wetter than 0.15 m), the area under the ROC curve is 0.62 in 2020 and 2025 (0.5 = chance).

Switching on the polder pumps (section 3) lowered the window hit rate from 50 to 43 % in 2020 and from 47 to 45 % in 2021, left 2025 at 41 %, and reduced the 2020 flooded area from 83 to 67 km²; wet antecedent soil (AMC III) raised the hit rate slightly (51, 48 and 41 %) and the 2020 area to 84 km². Since the model is already too dry, the app starts with the pumps off and offers them as a scenario. This is an offset of errors, not evidence that pumps do not work (section 9). The earlier official Dinas SDA flood records per RT [@dsda2026] gave the same picture with the old rule (model shallower than recorded); Jakarta Satu could not be reached for the re-scoring.

Taken together, the model reproduces the regional pattern of where water collects better than chance in two of three floods, but it is too dry and too shallow at the places citizens report, and its agreement at the scale of single neighbourhoods is weak. Two known limits explain much of this. The satellite rain averages over 0.1° cells (section 9), and the vertical error of the open 30 m terrain, about 1 m in built-up areas [@hawker2022], is larger than typical urban flood depths of 0.3–0.7 m, so which street floods first is below what the terrain can resolve. Results should be read at the scale of districts and river corridors and as comparisons between scenarios.

The 2020 flood was also run once without the river inflow from the catchments upstream of the region, so that each flooded cell can be traced to its source. With local rain alone the model flooded 248 km² of land deeper than 0.15 m in the whole region, against 300 km² with the upstream inflow, and every cell flooded by local rain alone was also flooded with the inflow. Upstream river water therefore added 52 km², about one sixth of the flooded area, but nearly doubled the area deeper than 1 m from 15 to 26 km². Only 17 % of the flooded area lay within 90 m of a mapped channel, so most modelled flooding is ponding of local rain in low ground, while river water mainly deepens the floods along the river corridors (verification/observed/attribution-2020-01-01.json).

## 9. Limitations

Tertiary and micro drains carry no size in the Jakarta Satu records, sluice gates are not modelled, and the NCICD sea walls are mapped without crest heights. The remaining city drains were represented as a sink on built-up cells, proportional to the built share, and calibrated on the 2020 flood: every capacity from 1 to 40 mm/h lowered the share of reports hit and pushed the flooded area below the reported one, so the app uses none (scored with the earlier rule that counted channel water; the pumps were re-scored with the land-only rule of section 8 and gave the same direction). IMERG averages rain over 0.1° cells: the cell of the Halim gauge received 166 mm over the whole three-day window, while the gauge recorded 377 mm in one day [@lubis2022], so the missing drains and the smoothed rain can offset each other and one event cannot separate them. Multiplying the IMERG rain by 1.5 and 2 as a sensitivity test brought the median depth at the 2020 reports to 0.62 and 0.80 m, but raised the flooded area in the five cities to 200 and 239 km², above the 156 km² reported; the factor that matched the report depths differed between events (about 2 in 2021, below 1.5 in 2025). The shortfall at report points is therefore not a uniform rain error, and the app keeps the rain as observed. Sea walls are not represented, so above a sea level of about 0.5 m results overstate flooding along the coast. The terrain is shifted by InSAR subsidence rates for 2016–2024 extrapolated at a constant rate back to 2013 and forward to the simulated date, inside DKI Jakarta only; earlier rates were higher in places [@abidin2011], and subsidence in Tangerang, Bekasi and Depok is not included. Channel cross-sections below the recorded water surface are missing.

In the design-storm and typed options, upstream rain is spatially uniform and equal to the scenario rain, and a lumped unit hydrograph for the largest catchment (the Cisadane, about 1,100 km²) is a simplification of its response. Agreement with observed floods was measured at report points and for the flooded area of one event; the shapes of flooded neighbourhoods (RW) are not openly available, so false alarms could not be counted place by place.

## 10. Exposure

Buildings and people reached by a flood are counted on the 30 m model grid. Each building footprint of Overture Maps [@overture2026] counts once, in the cell holding its centroid. Each WorldPop 2025 count on its 100 m grid [@worldpop2025] is split evenly over the 30 m cells whose centres fall in that pixel, which keeps the totals. Kecamatan come from the Overture divisions (OpenStreetMap boundaries); Kota Bekasi and part of Depok have no kecamatan polygons there and are counted per city. A building or person counts as affected when the cell, or the coarser run cell holding it, is deeper than 0.15 m outside river channels, at the time shown or at the maximum. The counts say where people live, not who was present during the flood, and inherit the depth errors of section 8.
