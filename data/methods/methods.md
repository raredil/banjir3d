# Methods and scientific basis of the Banjir3D Explore engine

## 1. Purpose and scope

Banjir3D is a free, browser-based tool for exploring pluvial and fluvial flood scenarios in Jakarta, Indonesia. It is organised around two engines with different roles. The Explore engine described here runs entirely in the user's web browser and is meant for rapid what-if analysis, teaching and the selection of scenarios; the Research Run engine uses SFINCS, a peer-reviewed reduced-physics hydrodynamic model [@leijnse2021; @vanormondt2025], and is the intended source of any number reported in a publication. This separation follows a simple principle: a fast interactive model is useful only if its equations, inputs and limits are stated openly and if it has been checked against an established reference model, so every element below is tied either to the peer-reviewed literature or to an explicit test that the reader can repeat. Every Explore result is labelled as exploratory and must not be used for quantitative conclusions until it has been validated against observed floods.

## 2. Governing equations: the local inertial approximation

The Explore engine solves the two-dimensional shallow water equations in the local inertial form proposed by @bates2010. In this form the convective acceleration terms of the full momentum equation are neglected, while local acceleration, the water-surface slope and bed friction are retained. For the unit-width discharge q in the x direction, the momentum equation therefore takes the following form.

    ∂q/∂t + g h ∂η/∂x + g n² q |q| / h^(7/3) = 0

In this equation h is the water depth, η = z + h is the water-surface elevation above the bed elevation z, g is gravitational acceleration and n is Manning's roughness coefficient. The y direction is treated in the same way, and mass is conserved by ∂h/∂t + ∂q_x/∂x + ∂q_y/∂y = R − D, with R the rainfall rate and D a drainage loss. The approximation is appropriate for the slow, gradually varied, subcritical flow that dominates floodplain and urban inundation. Its range of validity was analysed by @dealmeida2013, who showed that it reproduces the full shallow water equations well for flows with low Froude numbers, while errors grow as the flow approaches critical conditions; flows on the flat, highly built-up plain of central Jakarta are expected to lie mostly in the low-Froude range, but steep local gradients, such as at embankment overtopping, are outside it. The same equations underlie the "ACC" solver of LISFLOOD-FP and, without the optional advection term, the solver of SFINCS [@bates2010; @leijnse2021], which is why SFINCS can serve as a like-for-like reference for the Explore engine.

## 3. Numerical scheme

The equations are discretised on a regular staggered grid, with depths stored at cell centres and discharges at cell faces. They are integrated in time with the explicit scheme of @bates2010, in which the friction term is treated semi-implicitly, so that the discharge at a face is updated with the following expression.

    q^(t+Δt) = [θ q^t + (1 − θ)/2 (q_left^t + q_right^t) − g h_f Δt (Δη/Δx)] / [1 + g Δt n² |q^t| / h_f^(7/3)]

In this expression h_f is the flow depth at the face, taken as the difference between the higher of the two water-surface elevations and the higher of the two bed elevations, and the friction term uses the magnitude of the full discharge vector at the face. The weighting factor θ was introduced by @dealmeida2012 to suppress the instabilities that the original scheme can develop in areas of low friction; θ = 1 recovers the original scheme of @bates2010. The Explore engine uses θ = 1, which is the default of SFINCS, because the analytical test in Section 8 showed that θ = 0.7 delays the wetting front on an initially dry bed in our implementation, and because the comparison with SFINCS in Section 8 is only meaningful when both models use the same value.

The time step follows the stability condition derived by @bates2010 from the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy criterion, Δt = α Δx / √(g h_max), where h_max is the largest depth in the domain; the Explore engine uses α = 0.5 together with an upper limit of 15 s. A face carries flow only when h_f exceeds a wet/dry threshold of 0.05 m, the default minimum flow depth of SFINCS. Two further safeguards keep the solution physical: the face discharge is limited to critical flow (a Froude number of one), consistent with the subcritical range in which the approximation is valid [@dealmeida2013], and the outflow of every cell within a time step is scaled down, if necessary, so that no cell can lose more water than it holds. With these rules the scheme conserves mass to machine precision; the relative mass-balance error reported in the interface is typically of the order of 10⁻¹⁴.

## 4. Boundary conditions

The edges of the simulation area are either closed walls or open edges. At an open edge the engine applies a zero-gradient (transmissive) condition: the discharge leaving the domain equals the discharge arriving at the adjacent interior face, provided that it points outward, and it is limited to critical flow. This condition lets water leave without imposing an arbitrary downstream slope or water level, and it never lets water enter from outside. Its consequence is that an open box receives no runoff from the area around it, so the box should enclose the catchment that drains into the place of interest. A river inflow can be added as a constant discharge into the lowest cell of the southern edge and its two neighbours; this is a scenario assumption, not a gauged boundary condition. Tide and sea level at the coast are not yet represented in the Explore engine and are part of the planned SFINCS set-up.

## 5. Terrain

The terrain is FABDEM V1-2, a global 30 m digital elevation model derived from the Copernicus GLO-30 surface model from which forests and buildings have been removed with a machine-learning correction [@hawker2022]. A bare-earth model is the appropriate choice for a rain-on-grid model whose roughness term represents the effect of buildings (Section 6), because a surface model would turn every building into an obstacle at a resolution that cannot represent the streets between them. In the browser the elevations are read from the same tiles that are displayed on the map, on the Web Mercator pixel grid at zoom level 12; the ground size of a cell is the pixel size multiplied by the cosine of the latitude, about 38 m at Jakarta. For areas larger than about 160,000 cells, blocks of k × k pixels are averaged into one model cell so that a run stays interactive, and the cell size is reported in the interface. Vertical heights refer to the EGM2008 geoid. Three properties of the terrain limit the results: a 30 m model cannot resolve narrow canals, drains, embankments and walls; any vertical error of the model passes directly into the simulated depths; and Jakarta is subsiding at rates that vary strongly in space [@abidin2011], so the age of the elevation data matters. The national 8 m DEMNAS model of Badan Informasi Geospasial is planned as an alternative input.

## 6. Surface roughness

Manning's roughness coefficient is assigned per cell from the ESA WorldCover 2021 land-cover map at 10 m resolution [@zanaga2022]. Each land-cover class receives a value from the standard tables of @chow1959, @arcement1989 and the HEC-RAS two-dimensional modelling manual [@hecras2d]; the value of a model cell is the mean of the values of all 10 m pixels inside it, and the value at a face is the mean of its two neighbouring cells, as in SFINCS. The class "built-up" needs particular care because the terrain is bare earth. Buildings are therefore represented through an increased effective roughness, an approach that @beretta2018 found to be about as accurate as an explicit representation of the building geometry in a laboratory model of an urban district. The typical value of 0.15 s m⁻¹ᐟ³ and the sensitivity range of 0.12 to 0.20 s m⁻¹ᐟ³ are those given by the HEC-RAS manual for high-intensity developed land [@hecras2d]. The same manual notes that its values apply to appreciable flow depths and that shallow overland flow requires higher values, so depths of a few centimetres should be read with caution. The interface offers the typical, low and high values of every class, as well as a uniform n of 0.05 s m⁻¹ᐟ³, so that the sensitivity of a result to roughness can be shown directly. None of these values has been calibrated for Jakarta.

## 7. Rainfall and losses

Rain is applied directly to every cell of the grid, the so-called rain-on-grid or direct-rainfall approach, which is also the way SFINCS represents pluvial forcing [@leijnse2021]. Three rainfall inputs are offered. The first is a design storm built from a 24-hour rainfall total R24 with the Mononobe intensity–duration relation, I_t = (R24/24)(24/t)^(2/3), a relation widely used in Indonesian practice when only daily rainfall totals are available, which @maitsa2021 compared for Jakarta with observed intensities and with the procedure of the national standard for design flood computation [@bsn2016]; the cumulative depth of the most intense t hours is therefore R24 (t/24)^(1/3), and the hourly increments are arranged with the alternating block method, placing the largest block in the middle of the storm and the following blocks alternately to its right and left [@chow1988]. The second input is a series of hourly rainfall depths typed by the user, for example from a rain gauge, and the third is a constant intensity, which is used for the model comparison in Section 8.

The default scenario is the documented event of 1 January 2020, when up to 377 mm of rain fell in one day at Halim Perdanakusuma in East Jakarta, the highest daily amount recorded in the city since 1866 [@lubis2022]. Because the hourly sequence of that day is not part of the cited record, the daily total is distributed over 24 hours with the design-storm shape described above, and the result should be read as a design-storm interpretation of the event rather than as a reconstruction of its timing. This distinction matters, because an analysis of hourly records at Kemayoran (Jakarta) and Citeko (Bogor) by @maitsa2021 found that most rain events last less than four hours, that the largest share of the rain falls in the first two hours, and that intensities estimated with the alternating block and modified Mononobe methods differ from the observed ones by more than 14 percent on average. For this reason the typed hourly input should be preferred whenever gauge or radar data for an event are available.

Infiltration is not modelled, which is a conservative simplification for the central Jakarta sample, of which about 87 percent is built-up land according to WorldCover. The capacity of the sewer and drainage network is represented, if at all, by a uniform drainage loss in millimetres per hour that the user sets; its default is zero, which describes the upper-bound case of no drainage, because the capacity of the Jakarta drainage network is not documented in an open, citable source.

## 8. Verification

Verification asks whether the code solves its equations correctly; it is a necessary step before, but not a substitute for, validation against observed floods. The engine is verified in three ways, and all tests run automatically with every change to the code. The first group of tests checks basic properties: a lake at rest stays at rest on a flat and on a sloping bed, mass is conserved to better than 10⁻⁹ with rain, drainage and open edges, depths never become negative, and a steady inflow leaves through an open edge at the rate at which it enters.

The second test uses the analytical solution of @hunter2005 for a flood wave that advances at a constant velocity u over a horizontal, initially dry plane, h(x,t) = [−(7/3) n² u² (x − u t)]^(3/7), which @dealmeida2013 used to test the local inertial scheme. With u = 1 m/s, n = 0.03 s m⁻¹ᐟ³ and 50 m cells, the simulated depth profile after one hour has a root-mean-square error of 0.024 m, 1.0 percent of the 2.38 m depth imposed at the inflow boundary, and the wetting front is within one cell of its analytical position. Over the other combinations tested (n from 0.01 to 0.15 s m⁻¹ᐟ³ and cells from 12.5 to 100 m) the error lies between 1.0 and 6.1 percent of the boundary depth with θ = 1. The largest differences occur near the thin wetting front and at low friction, and they do not shrink when the grid is refined, because the analytical solution neglects inertia whereas the local inertial equations retain the local acceleration term; near the front the Froude number is high, which is exactly where @dealmeida2013 expected the two formulations to differ. The scripts and results are kept in the repository, so the numbers can be reproduced.

The third test compares the Explore engine with SFINCS version 2.4.0, compiled from the published source code, on the 10 × 10 km Ciliwung sample with the same terrain, roughness and rain (100 mm/h for three hours, eight hours simulated, closed edges, advection switched off in SFINCS). With a uniform n of 0.05 the maximum depths of the two models differ by a root-mean-square error of 0.039 m with a correlation of 0.998, and the Critical Success Index of the flooded area is 0.955 for depths above 0.3 m; with the WorldCover roughness field the corresponding values are 0.056 m, 0.995 and 0.951. The stored volumes differ by at most 0.4 percent during the run. The Critical Success Index is reported with the caution that binary measures of this kind depend on the size of the flood and are not comparable between events of different magnitude [@stephens2014]. This is a model-to-model comparison on a closed domain with shared inputs: it shows that the browser solver reproduces the reference solver, but it says nothing about the accuracy of the shared inputs. The next verification steps are the standard two-dimensional benchmark tests of the UK Environment Agency [@neelz2013] and comparisons with open boundaries and river inflow.

## 9. Limitations and intended use

The Explore engine has not yet been validated against observed flood extents or depths, and its results are labelled accordingly. Its main physical limitations are the 30 m bare-earth terrain, which does not resolve canals, drains and embankments; the uncalibrated literature roughness; the absence of infiltration, of the sewer and pump network, of tide and of the river inflow from the upstream catchments, except as user-set scenario values; the uniform spatial distribution of rain; and the land subsidence that has occurred since the terrain data were acquired. Its numerical limitations are the wet/dry threshold of 0.05 m, which makes shallow-flood extents less reliable than deeper ones, and the local inertial approximation itself, which is not suited to supercritical or rapidly varied flow. Within these limits the engine is a transparent and repeatable tool for exploring how rainfall, roughness and drainage assumptions change the pattern of flooding, and for choosing scenarios that are then run with SFINCS and validated against observations before any quantitative conclusion is drawn.
