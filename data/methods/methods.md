# Methods of the Banjir3D flood engine (version 2)

This text describes only what the running system does. Every number the engine uses is either read from a named dataset or fixed by a cited method; the user sets only external forcing (rain, sea level and simulated time).

## 1. Model domain and grid

The model grid is the FABDEM V1-2 bare-earth terrain [@hawker2022] reprojected to WGS 84 / UTM zone 48S at 30 m, covering mainland DKI Jakarta plus a 10 km buffer (about 52 × 51 km, 2.98 million cells). Cells outside the buffer polygon are inactive, and a run can also use a user-drawn box or blocks of 2 × 2 or 3 × 3 cells (60 m or 90 m) for faster runs.

A drawn box can also run on cells of 10, 5, 2 or 1 m. The terrain has no detail below 30 m, so the ground of a fine cell is interpolated bilinearly between the centres of the neighbouring land cells, channel cells keep their own bed level, and roughness, curve number and sea and channel flags are taken from the 30 m cell that holds the fine cell. A river entering the box from upstream (section 5) enters over all open cells of its 30 m entry cell, so the entry is as wide as on the 30 m grid. The detail that is real at this size is the building footprints of Overture Maps [@overture2026]: a fine cell whose centre lies inside a footprint is raised 10 m as a solid block, so water flows in the streets between buildings and rain on a roof runs off to the street (the building-block method of @schubert2012). Open cells closed in on all sides by buildings, such as courtyards and alleys narrower than the cell, join the block, so their rain also reaches the street rather than filling a pond that only the cell size created (at 10 m about 8 % of the open cells in a test box in Kampung Melayu, at 2 m about 1 %). Because the buildings are now in the grid, the open ground between them takes the roughness of a street (n = 0.016, rough asphalt in @chow1959) in place of the built-area share's n = 0.15, which on 30 m cells stands for those same buildings (section 4); keeping 0.15 would count the buildings twice and made water in the streets too slow and too deep. On a 1 km box on the Ciliwung at Kampung Melayu (150 mm in 6 h, upstream inflow), the flooded land deeper than 0.15 m was 0.065 km² at 30 m, 0.08–0.10 km² at 10 m, 0.03–0.05 km² at 5 m and 0.028 km² at 2 m; before these corrections it ranged from 0.03 to 0.41 km² with the cell size. The remaining spread comes from the buildings themselves: at 10 m narrow streets close up, from 5 m down the results converge (`verification/fine-consistency/`). A fine run therefore changes where water goes between buildings, but it adds no terrain information, and it is not part of the calibration of section 8.

Fine runs use a dual grid [@sanders2019] so that small cells stay fast. The time step of an explicit scheme shrinks with the cell size and with the deepest water (section 2), so a 1 m grid needs about 30 times more steps than a 30 m grid on 900 times more cells. The flow is therefore solved on the finest compute grid (a divisor of the fine cell size: 30, 15, 10, 6, 5, 3 or 2 m) whose estimated run time, cells times steps divided by the speed this browser measured on its previous run (default 12 million cell updates per second on the CPU, 300 million with WebGPU), fits about 30 s; when the fine grid itself fits, it is solved cell by cell with building blocks as above. A coarser compute cell carries its fine cells as building porosity [@sanders2008]: the open share of the cell stores water, the conveyance between two cell centres is the harmonic mean of the open share of each fine column (or row) in between, so a closed row of buildings blocks the flow, and it never exceeds the open share of either cell, which keeps the explicit update stable. Rain on roofs reaches the open ground of the same cell; a cell with less than 10 % open ground hands it to the nearest ring of cells with open ground (up to 3 cells away). Cells sealed on all sides by buildings do not limit the time step. The water surface of the compute cells is then drawn on every fine cell, as depth above the fine cell's ground, with no water on building cells; exports use the same fine cells. Against the building-block grid at 2 m on a 250 m box in Johar Baru (no channel, 44 % building cover, 377 mm design storm, first 14 h), porosity on 6 m and 10 m compute cells ran 37 and 177 times faster, with a mean depth difference of −2 cm, depth RMSE 0.20–0.25 m and a critical success index of 0.64–0.72 for cells deeper than 0.15 m; the porosity grid is somewhat drier (flooded share 0.54–0.56 against 0.62) because water leaves the box faster, and the building-block grid traps rain in closed courtyards (`verification/fine-speed/`).

## 2. Hydrodynamics

The engine solves the two-dimensional shallow water equations in the local inertial form of @bates2010, the scheme used by LISFLOOD-FP and, without advection, by SFINCS [@leijnse2021; @vanormondt2025]. The momentum equation for the unit-width discharge q in each direction is the following.

    ∂q/∂t + g h ∂η/∂x + g n² q |q| / h^(7/3) = 0

Here h is depth, η = z + h the water level, g gravity and n Manning's coefficient; friction is treated semi-implicitly and the face depth is the difference between the higher water level and the higher bed of the two cells, as in @bates2010. The local inertial form is accurate for the low-Froude flow typical of urban floodplains and loses accuracy near critical flow [@dealmeida2013], so face discharge is capped at the critical value h√(gh).

The time step follows the Courant condition Δt = α Δx / √(g h_max) with α = 0.5 and an upper limit of 15 s. Mass is conserved by updating depth from the net face fluxes, and a limiter scales outgoing fluxes so that no cell gives away more water than it holds. A face carries flow only when its depth exceeds 0.001 m, the default threshold of LISFLOOD-FP, so thin runoff sheets still drain.

## 3. Terrain conditioning with mapped rivers

OpenStreetMap river and canal polygons and river, canal and stream centre lines (snapshot of October 2026) are rasterised onto the grid. Each channel cell takes the lowest terrain value within one cell, which is the water surface the DEM recorded, and along each centre line a one-dimensional morphological opening over five cells (150 m) removes humps narrower than that window, such as bridges, culverts and road embankments that a DEM keeps as dams. No depth is added below the recorded water surface, because no open dataset gives channel bathymetry; the channel volume below that surface is taken as already filled by base flow at the start of an event.

Primary and secondary drains of the Dinas Sumber Daya Air of DKI Jakarta (Jakarta Satu geoportal) [@dsda2026], which record width and depth for each channel, are added as channel cells. Where a depth is given, the bed is lowered to the bank level (the median of the surrounding 3 × 3 terrain) minus the depth scaled by the share of the cell the channel occupies (depth × width / 30 m, width at most 30 m), unless the terrain is already lower. The carved part below the water surface the DEM recorded starts filled with water, as for the OpenStreetMap channels, so the drains add conveyance rather than empty storage.

The same agency maps 263 pump stations with their capacity (m³/s) and 65 polder systems. Each pump serves the polder it stands in, or the nearest polder within 500 m, since stations sit on the polder edge. The summed capacity of a polder is spread evenly over its land cells (not channels or sea) as a sink rate, so a fully wet polder loses exactly its pump capacity and a partly wet one loses less: the pumps are a lower bound. Pumped water leaves the model. The app can switch the pumps off as a scenario.

Land subsidence moves the terrain after it was measured. The FABDEM terrain comes from Copernicus DEM radar acquisitions of 2011–2015, taken here as the epoch 2013.0. Subsidence rates come from the Sinking City dataset [@shabrina2026]: annual Sentinel-1 line-of-sight velocities for 2016–2024 from LiCSBAS [@morishita2020] on LiCSAR interferograms of ascending frame 098A_09673_121312 [@lazecky2020], kriged to 30 m. Single years are noisy, so each cell takes the mean of the nine annual velocities, divided by the up component of the line-of-sight vector (0.77–0.79 over Jakarta, from the LiCSAR frame metadata) to give vertical motion, assuming horizontal motion is negligible; upward values are set to zero, as the dataset authors do in their maps. Before each run the terrain of every cell is lowered by its rate times the years between 2013.0 and the simulated date (the event date for an observed flood, today for a scenario). The map covers DKI Jakarta only; elsewhere the rate is zero.

DEMNAS, the 8 m national DEM of Badan Informasi Geospasial, is shown on the map but is not the model terrain. Averaged to the 30 m grid, it lies a median 0.79 m above FABDEM in built-up cells, and regressed on the excess of the Copernicus surface model over FABDEM it keeps a slope of 0.62, that is about 60 % of building and tree height; water is filled at a constant level near −3.3 m.

## 4. Land cover, roughness and infiltration

Land cover is the Esri / Impact Observatory 10 m annual map of the newest available year, 2024 at build time. Each 30 m cell receives the area-weighted Manning n of its nine 10 m pixels, using HEC-RAS land-classification values after @chow1959 [@hecras2d]; built-up land uses n = 0.15, an effective roughness that represents buildings removed from the bare-earth DEM, an approach @beretta2018 found about as accurate as explicit building geometry. Mapped channel cells (section 3) take the open-water value n = 0.035 instead, the upper end of the range for clean natural and excavated channels in @chow1959: a river is often narrower than the 10 m land-cover pixels, so its own cell would otherwise carry the roughness of the buildings along its banks. Until November 2026 they did, which made every channel about four times too rough; rivers then spilled over their banks on 30 m cells but stayed in them on 10 m cells, so results changed with the cell size (`verification/fine-consistency/`). A coarse cell that holds a channel keeps the channel's level and roughness.

Rain becomes runoff through the NRCS curve number method [@nrcs1986]. For cumulative rain P and retention S = 25400/CN − 254 (mm), direct runoff is Q = (P − 0.2S)² / (P + 0.8S) for P > 0.2S and zero otherwise; each step adds the increase of Q to the cell, so the rest of the rain infiltrates. Curve numbers come from TR-55 tables for each land-cover class and hydrologic soil group, built-up pixels count as impervious (CN 98) and a cell takes the area-weighted value of its pixels, which is the TR-55 composite curve number for mixed cells.

The hydrologic soil group is derived from SoilGrids 2.0 sand and clay contents averaged over 0–100 cm [@poggio2021], using the texture criteria of the NRCS soil-group chapter [@nrcs2009] that also underlie the global HYSOGs250m map [@ross2018]. In the Jakarta region the result is 83 % group D and 17 % group C, consistent with the clay-rich soils of the area. Antecedent moisture is the average condition (AMC II) of the method.

## 5. Inflow from upstream catchments

Flow directions are computed on the Copernicus GLO-90 DEM over 106.35–107.35° E and 5.95–7.00° S, which holds every catchment draining into the region. Mapped rivers and streams of the whole area (Overture Maps base theme, from OpenStreetMap) are lowered by 20 m before routing so that the D8 directions follow the mapped network on flat land, and canals are left out because they cross catchment divides. For every stream cell draining at least 1 km² the engine stores upstream area, area-mean curve number, mean slope and longest flow length.

When a run starts, the engine finds every stream that crosses into the run area and computes its inflow hydrograph from the same rain applied over its catchment. Runoff depth follows the curve number method, the lag time follows the NRCS watershed lag equation [@nrcs2010] and the discharge follows the NRCS dimensionless unit hydrograph [@nrcs2007], integrated over 5-minute steps so that the runoff volume is kept exactly. Catchment area that lies inside the run area, or that was already counted at an upstream entry, is removed so that no rain is counted twice.

## 6. Rain and sea level

The design-storm option distributes a 24-hour rain total R24 over the chosen duration with the Mononobe intensity–duration relation used in Indonesian practice [@maitsa2021] and the alternating block method [@chow1988]. For scale, Halim Perdanakusuma recorded 377 mm on 1 January 2020 [@lubis2022]; users can also type hourly depths. The observed-flood option replays the three floods of section 8 with the hourly GPM IMERG rain of every 0.1° cell [@huffman2023]: each model cell and each upstream catchment takes the rain of its nearest cell centre, as in the validation runs.

Sea cells, defined as water pixels at or below 0.5 m connected to the open sea, are held at the chosen sea level throughout a run. Water reaching any other edge of the run area leaves through a transmissive boundary that passes the discharge arriving at the adjacent face, capped at critical flow.

## 7. Display and export

The map shows depth per pixel by interpolating the water level bilinearly from the surrounding wet cells and subtracting the ground at that pixel, the water-level downscaling used for SFINCS results [@leijnse2021]. The ground follows the same rule on every grid: inside a channel cell it is the channel bed, elsewhere it is interpolated bilinearly between the neighbouring land cells only, so the bed of a river is not spread onto its banks (before November 2026 the 30 m map did spread it, which drew a band of water along rivers that stayed within their banks), and fine runs show no water on building cells. This gives smooth water edges that follow the ground without inventing water where all surrounding cells are dry, and colours follow the depth classes 0.05, 0.15, 0.5, 1 and 2 m. Depth exports are GeoTIFF files in EPSG:32748 with the model values unchanged, together with a JSON record of all inputs, data versions, inflows and the water balance.

## 8. Verification

The WebGPU solver gives the same maximum depths as the CPU reference on real terrain to within 0.003 m, and both close the water balance to within 10⁻⁷ of the inflow. The scheme reproduces the analytical flood wave of @hunter2005 within 3 % of the inflow depth and matched SFINCS 2.4.0 on the same grid, rain and roughness with an RMSE of 0.06 m in maximum depth. These tests check the numerics and say nothing about agreement with real floods.

The engine was then run on the whole region at 90 m for three observed floods: 1 January 2020 for calibration, and 20 February 2021 and 4 March 2025 for validation. Rain came from the half-hourly GPM IMERG Final estimates on their 0.1° cells [@huffman2023], each upstream catchment receiving the rain of its own cell. A citizen flood report from PetaBencana.id counts as hit when the largest maximum depth within one model cell of the report reaches 0.10 m. Without a drainage sink the model hit 80 % of 259 reports in 2020, 78 % of 372 in 2021 and 81 % of 47 in 2025. In 2020 it flooded 143 km² of land deeper than 0.15 m inside the five mainland cities, against the 156 km² and 390 RW the city government reported for that flood. Modelled depths at the reports were shallower than reported, with medians of 0.36 m against 0.70 m in 2020, 0.25 m against 0.65 m in 2021 and 0.27 m against 0.55 m in 2025.

The same three floods were run again after the Jakarta Satu drains were carved into the terrain and the terrain was lowered by the subsidence rates (section 3). The median modelled depth at the reports rose from 0.36 to 0.38 m in 2020 (reported 0.70 m), from 0.25 to 0.38 m in 2021 (0.65 m) and from 0.27 to 0.43 m in 2025 (0.55 m). The share of reports hit stayed at 78 % in 2021 and 81 % in 2025 but fell from 80 % to 72 % in 2020, and the flooded area in the five cities in 2020 fell from 143 to 87 km², further below the 156 km² reported. Shifting the terrain to the event date changed the results by less than 1 km² and no report score: the subsidence accumulated since 2013 is small next to the flood depths. The official flood records of the Dinas SDA per RT dated in the event windows [@dsda2026] give a second, independent check: the model reaches 0.10 m in 64 % of 148 records in 2020, 46 % of 37 in 2021 and 56 % of 130 in 2025, with a median modelled depth of 0.17, 0.10 and 0.17 m where the records state 0.30, 0.40 and 0.60 m. Both sources agree that the model finds most flooded places but underestimates the depth. Switching on the polder pumps (section 3) lowered the share of reports hit from 72 to 65 % in 2020 and from 78 to 77 % in 2021, left 2025 at 81 %, and reduced the 2020 flooded area from 87 to 68 km²; since the model is already too dry, the app starts with the pumps off and offers them as a scenario switch. Place by place, over 2,610 RW polygons of the Sinking City dataset [@shabrina2026], an RW counts as observed flooded when it holds a citizen report or touches an official flood record of the event, and as modelled flooded when at least 10 % of its land is wetter than 0.15 m. The model flagged 44, 26 and 44 % of the observed RW in 2020, 2021 and 2025, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.57 to 0.61, and 82 to 91 % of the RW it flagged had no record. Records are incomplete, so the last figure is an upper bound on false alarms, but agreement at the scale of single neighbourhoods is weak and results should be read at the scale of districts and river corridors.

The 2020 flood was also run once without the river inflow from the catchments upstream of the region, so that each flooded cell can be traced to its source. With local rain alone the model flooded 248 km² of land deeper than 0.15 m in the whole region, against 300 km² with the upstream inflow, and every cell flooded by local rain alone was also flooded with the inflow. Upstream river water therefore added 52 km², about one sixth of the flooded area, but nearly doubled the area deeper than 1 m from 15 to 26 km². Only 17 % of the flooded area lay within 90 m of a mapped channel, so most modelled flooding is ponding of local rain in low ground, while river water mainly deepens the floods along the river corridors (verification/observed/attribution-2020-01-01.json).

## 9. Limitations

Tertiary and micro drains carry no size in the Jakarta Satu records, sluice gates are not modelled, and the NCICD sea walls are mapped without crest heights. The remaining city drains were represented as a sink on built-up cells, proportional to the built share, and calibrated on the 2020 flood: every capacity from 1 to 40 mm/h lowered the share of reports hit and pushed the flooded area below the reported one, so the app uses none. IMERG averages rain over 0.1° cells: the cell of the Halim gauge received 166 mm over the whole three-day window, while the gauge recorded 377 mm in one day [@lubis2022], so the missing drains and the smoothed rain can offset each other and one event cannot separate them. Multiplying the IMERG rain by 1.5 and 2 as a sensitivity test brought the median depth at the 2020 reports to 0.62 and 0.80 m, but raised the flooded area in the five cities to 200 and 239 km², above the 156 km² reported; the factor that matched the report depths differed between events (about 2 in 2021, below 1.5 in 2025). The shortfall at report points is therefore not a uniform rain error, and the app keeps the rain as observed. Sea walls are not represented, so above a sea level of about 0.5 m results overstate flooding along the coast. The terrain is shifted by InSAR subsidence rates for 2016–2024 extrapolated at a constant rate back to 2013 and forward to the simulated date, inside DKI Jakarta only; earlier rates were higher in places [@abidin2011], and subsidence in Tangerang, Bekasi and Depok is not included. Channel cross-sections below the recorded water surface are missing.

In the design-storm and typed options, upstream rain is spatially uniform and equal to the scenario rain, and a lumped unit hydrograph for the largest catchment (the Cisadane, about 1,100 km²) is a simplification of its response. Agreement with observed floods was measured at report points and for the flooded area of one event; the shapes of flooded neighbourhoods (RW) are not openly available, so false alarms could not be counted place by place.

## 10. Exposure

Buildings and people reached by a flood are counted on the 30 m model grid. Each building footprint of Overture Maps [@overture2026] counts once, in the cell holding its centroid. Each WorldPop 2025 count on its 100 m grid [@worldpop2025] is split evenly over the 30 m cells whose centres fall in that pixel, which keeps the totals. Kecamatan come from the Overture divisions (OpenStreetMap boundaries); Kota Bekasi and part of Depok have no kecamatan polygons there and are counted per city. A building or person counts as affected when the cell, or the coarser run cell holding it, is deeper than 0.15 m outside river channels, at the time shown or at the maximum. The counts say where people live, not who was present during the flood, and inherit the depth errors of section 8.
