# Methods of the Banjir3D flood engine (version 2)

This text describes only what the running system does. Every number the engine uses is either read from a named dataset or fixed by a cited method; the user sets only external forcing (rain, sea level and simulated time).

## 1. Model domain and grid

The model grid is the FABDEM V1-2 bare-earth terrain [@hawker2022] reprojected to WGS 84 / UTM zone 48S at 30 m, covering mainland DKI Jakarta plus a 10 km buffer (about 52 × 51 km, 2.98 million cells). Cells outside the buffer polygon are inactive, and a run can also use a user-drawn box or blocks of 2 × 2 or 3 × 3 cells (60 m or 90 m) for faster runs.

A drawn box can also be shown on cells of 10, 5, 2 or 1 m. No open bare-earth terrain is finer than 30 m here, so the flow is solved on the same 30 m grid as a 30 m run of the box, the grid checked against recorded floods (section 8), and the fine cells only draw its result: the water surface of the 30 m cells, interpolated bilinearly, minus the land surface interpolated bilinearly to the fine cell. Area, depth, buildings and people affected are therefore identical at 30 m and on every finer cell size; only the drawing and the exported rasters are finer. Until November 2026 building footprints were cut out of the drawn water and a trial option solved the flow among the buildings on the fine cells (building blocks, @schubert2012; building porosity on a dual grid, @sanders2008 and @sanders2019). The cut-outs made the map look fragmented without changing a number, and the trial flow put the flooding in other places than the 30 m run (critical success index 0.06–0.17 against 30 m, `verification/fine-consistency/`) because below 30 m the ground is only interpolated and the buildings take over from the built-area roughness on which the checks rest, so both were removed from the app (ADR 0019; the code stays for the verification scripts).

Cells of 60 and 90 m average the 30 m terrain over blocks of 2 × 2 and 3 × 3 cells; a block that holds a channel keeps the channel's level and roughness so the river stays connected. Since 20 November 2026 such a block also keeps the mean level and the share of its land (non-channel) cells: flooded area, buildings, people, the map and the validation scores count the water above that land level on that land share, and its start water is the block mean (the same volume as at 30 m). Before, the whole block counted as river: at 90 m 241 km² of 'channel' against 107 km² at 30 m, so riverside land was never counted as flooded and the start water was 2.3 times the 30 m volume (review 2026-11-20, verification/review-2026-11-18/01-metode.md M-04, M-05). Averaging smooths hollows, river banks and raised roads, so results differ somewhat between 30, 60 and 90 m, as in any grid model; 30 m is the reference, and 90 m is used for the whole-region checks of section 8 because it needs up to about 27 times less computing (nine times fewer cells, three times longer time steps).

## 2. Hydrodynamics

The engine solves the two-dimensional shallow water equations in the local inertial form of @bates2010, the scheme used by LISFLOOD-FP and, without advection, by SFINCS [@leijnse2021; @vanormondt2025]. The momentum equation for the unit-width discharge q in each direction is the following.

    ∂q/∂t + g h ∂η/∂x + g n² q |q| / h^(7/3) = 0

Here h is depth, η = z + h the water level, g gravity and n Manning's coefficient; friction is treated semi-implicitly and the face depth is the difference between the higher water level and the higher bed of the two cells, as in @bates2010. The local inertial form is accurate for the low-Froude flow typical of urban floodplains and loses accuracy near critical flow [@dealmeida2013], so face discharge is capped at the critical value h√(gh).

The time step follows the Courant condition Δt = α Δx / √(g h_max) with α = 0.7, the value used by LISFLOOD-FP, and an upper limit of 15 s. Until November 2026 the engine used α = 0.5; on a 4.4 km box at 30 m and on the whole region at 90 m the two give the same maximum depths (root-mean-square difference 1–6 mm, critical success index 0.999 for cells deeper than 0.15 m) and α = 0.7 runs about 30 % faster (`verification/speed/`). Mass is conserved by updating depth from the net face fluxes, and a limiter scales outgoing fluxes so that no cell gives away more water than it holds. A face carries flow only when its depth exceeds 0.001 m, the default threshold of LISFLOOD-FP, so thin runoff sheets still drain.

## 3. Terrain conditioning with mapped rivers

OpenStreetMap river and canal polygons and river, canal and stream centre lines (snapshot of October 2026) are rasterised onto the grid. Each channel cell takes the lowest terrain value within one cell, which is the water surface the DEM recorded, and along each centre line a one-dimensional morphological opening over five cells (150 m) removes humps narrower than that window, such as bridges, culverts and road embankments that a DEM keeps as dams. No depth is added below the recorded water surface, because no open dataset gives channel bathymetry; the channel volume below that surface is taken as already filled by base flow at the start of an event. Where a river is narrower than a cell this leaves it shallow: from bed to the median of the land within 90 m, mapped channel cells hold a median 1.0 m (Kali Baru 0.3 m, Mampang 0.8 m, Angke 1.6 m), against several metres in reality, so small rivers spill too early; this is a known limitation (section 9). Permanent water bodies (lakes, reservoirs, ponds and fishponds: at least half of the cell is open water in the land-cover map, and not sea) are flagged like channels from the pack of 17 November 2026: they always hold water, so their water is not counted or drawn as a flood. Before, they were counted as flooded land, with a mean depth of 1.3 m in the 1 January 2020 run, and showed as the deepest floods on open ground.

Primary and secondary drains of the Dinas Sumber Daya Air of DKI Jakarta (Jakarta Satu geoportal) [@dsda2026], which record width and depth for each channel, are added as channel cells. Where a depth is given, the bed is lowered to the bank level (the median of the surrounding 3 × 3 terrain) minus the depth scaled by the share of the cell the channel occupies (depth × width / 30 m, width at most 30 m), unless the terrain is already lower. The carved part below the water surface the DEM recorded starts filled with water, as for the OpenStreetMap channels, but only where the satellite saw water in the cell (at least half of the 30 m cell is open water in the land-cover map). Most of these drains are narrower than a cell, so there the DEM height is the bank or the street, not a water surface; there the drain starts empty above mean sea level and filled up to 0 m below it, since the drains of the low north are tidal and the model sea is held at 0 m by default. Until 17 November 2026 every carved cell started filled up to that level: 95 % of them were cells where the satellite saw land, holding a median 3.2 m of water (68 million m³ in all), which left a median 0.28 m of room before the bank and made the drains spill at the start of every run.

The same agency maps 263 pump stations with their capacity (m³/s) and 65 polder systems. Each pump serves the polder it stands in, or the nearest polder within 500 m, since stations sit on the polder edge. The summed capacity of a polder is spread evenly over its land cells (not channels or sea) as a sink rate, so a fully wet polder loses exactly its pump capacity and a partly wet one loses less: the pumps are a lower bound. Pumped water leaves the model. The app can switch the pumps off as a scenario.

Micro drains of South Jakarta (Jakarta Selatan) come from a Dinas SDA survey file (Jakarta Satu update 2024, cut 3 November 2026) supplied by the project owner [@dsda2026mikro]: 12,481 street-drain sections, of which 311 marked as removed in 2024 are left out and 8,551 (about 1,900 km) carry a width and depth (median 0.5 × 0.5 m). Each sized section has a full-flow capacity from Manning's equation for a rectangle w × d, with wetted perimeter w + 2d for open and 2(w + d) for covered drains, n from the lining (precast U-ditch 0.013, cast concrete and brick 0.015, cemented rubble masonry 0.025, other or unknown 0.020; @chow1959) and the bed slope taken as the slope of the 30 m terrain smoothed over 270 m and kept within 0.1–2 %, because the terrain cannot resolve the fall of one street drain. Sections whose ends lie within 10 m of another section form a network (444 networks). A network empties through its outlets, the sections that touch a channel cell (a river or a carved DSDA drain); its capacity is the sum of its outlet capacities, or its largest section when no section touches a channel. Every land cell within 100 m of a sized section is served by the network of the nearest section, and the network capacity spread over the land it serves is the cell's sink rate: the rational method read backwards, the steady rain the network can carry with all of it reaching the drains [@chow1988]. The result covers 115 km² at a mean of 38 mm/h (median 29 mm/h, 10–90 % range 6–73 mm/h). Internal bottlenecks, blocked or silted drains, inlets and backwater from a full river are not represented, so the rate is an upper bound of what the drains take, and drained water leaves the model rather than joining the river it empties into. The micro drains are a switch in the app, off by default after the test in section 8; sections without a size and the rest of Jakarta are not represented.

Land subsidence moves the terrain after it was measured. The FABDEM terrain comes from Copernicus DEM radar acquisitions of 2011–2015, taken here as the epoch 2013.0. Subsidence rates come from the Sinking City dataset [@shabrina2026]: annual Sentinel-1 line-of-sight velocities for 2016–2024 from LiCSBAS [@morishita2020] on LiCSAR interferograms of ascending frame 098A_09673_121312 [@lazecky2020], kriged to 30 m. Single years are noisy, so each cell takes the mean of the nine annual velocities, divided by the up component of the line-of-sight vector (0.77–0.79 over Jakarta, from the LiCSAR frame metadata) to give vertical motion, assuming horizontal motion is negligible; upward values are set to zero, as the dataset authors do in their maps. Before each run the terrain of every cell is lowered by its rate times the years between 2013.0 and the simulated date (the event date for an observed flood, today for a scenario). The map covers DKI Jakarta only; elsewhere the rate is zero.

DEMNAS, the 8 m national DEM of Badan Informasi Geospasial, is shown on the map but is not the model terrain. Averaged to the 30 m grid, it lies a median 0.79 m above FABDEM in built-up cells, and regressed on the excess of the Copernicus surface model over FABDEM it keeps a slope of 0.62, that is about 60 % of building and tree height; water is filled at a constant level near −3.3 m.

## 4. Land cover, roughness and infiltration

Land cover is the Esri / Impact Observatory 10 m annual map of the newest available year, 2024 at build time. Each 30 m cell receives the area-weighted Manning n of its nine 10 m pixels, using HEC-RAS land-classification values after @chow1959 [@hecras2d]; built-up land uses n = 0.15, an effective roughness that represents buildings removed from the bare-earth DEM, an approach @beretta2018 found about as accurate as explicit building geometry. Mapped channel cells (section 3) take the open-water value n = 0.035 instead, the upper end of the range for clean natural and excavated channels in @chow1959: a river is often narrower than the 10 m land-cover pixels, so its own cell would otherwise carry the roughness of the buildings along its banks. Until November 2026 they did, which made every channel about four times too rough; rivers then spilled over their banks on 30 m cells but stayed in them on 10 m cells, so results changed with the cell size (`verification/fine-consistency/`). A coarse cell that holds a channel keeps the channel's level and roughness.

Rain becomes runoff through the NRCS curve number method [@nrcs1986]. For cumulative rain P and retention S = 25400/CN − 254 (mm), direct runoff is Q = (P − 0.2S)² / (P + 0.8S) for P > 0.2S and zero otherwise; each step adds the increase of Q to the cell, so the rest of the rain infiltrates. Curve numbers come from TR-55 tables for each land-cover class and hydrologic soil group, built-up pixels count as impervious (CN 98) and a cell takes the area-weighted value of its pixels, which is the TR-55 composite curve number for mixed cells.

The hydrologic soil group is derived from SoilGrids 2.0 sand and clay contents averaged over 0–100 cm [@poggio2021], using the texture criteria of the NRCS soil-group chapter [@nrcs2009] that also underlie the global HYSOGs250m map [@ross2018]. Over the box that holds the upstream catchments the result is 83 % group D and 17 % group C, and inside the model domain 98 % D and 2 % C (engine pack of 14 November 2026), consistent with the clay-rich soils of the area: within Jakarta the soil barely varies, so land cover, not soil, sets where rain soaks in; the groups on the model grid are shown as the map layer "Kelompok tanah". SoilGrids is a 250 m prediction from soil profiles and covariates, not a field survey, and on built-up pixels the soil group does not matter (CN 98). A geological map of the Jakarta, Karawang, Serang and Anyer sheets supplied by the project owner (November 2026) shows that the soil groups are not uniform underneath: within the model domain 48 % of the land lies on alluvial fan deposits (volcanic sand and tuff), 22 % on flood-plain clay, 20 % on alluvium, 5 % on Banten tuff and 2 % on beach-ridge sand, so group D is likely too pessimistic for the fans, tuff and sand. As an upper bound, giving the fans and tuff group B, beach sand A, alluvium C and keeping the clays D lowers the runoff of a 150 mm storm over the domain land by only 1.0 % (140.5 to 139.1 mm), because 85 % of that land is built-up; the soil groups are therefore kept, and the geology matters more for the upstream catchments, which the map does not cover. Antecedent moisture is the average condition (AMC II) of the method, the condition used in all checks of section 8. The app can switch to the dry (AMC I) or wet (AMC III) condition, chosen by the rain of the five days before the storm (growing-season limits of 35.6 and 53.3 mm), with the curve numbers converted as CN(I) = 4.2 CN(II) / (10 − 0.058 CN(II)) and CN(III) = 23 CN(II) / (10 + 0.13 CN(II)) [@chow1988]; the conversion applies to the model cells and to the upstream catchments, while the catchment lag keeps the AMC II value. This is how rain of earlier days or weeks enters a run: a weekly or monthly total spread evenly over a simulation would be a drizzle that floods nothing and would ignore the storm days inside it. Infiltration can also be switched off (every drop runs off) as a what-if for sealed green space. In the model area 85 % of the land is built-up in the 2024 map and the mean curve number of the land cells is 97, so infiltration matters most in the upstream catchments.

## 5. Inflow from upstream catchments

Flow directions are computed on the Copernicus GLO-90 DEM over 106.35–107.35° E and 5.95–7.00° S, which holds every catchment draining into the region. Mapped rivers and streams of the whole area (Overture Maps base theme, from OpenStreetMap) are lowered by 20 m before routing so that the D8 directions follow the mapped network on flat land, and canals are left out because they cross catchment divides. For every stream cell draining at least 1 km² the engine stores upstream area, area-mean curve number, mean slope and longest flow length.

When a run starts, the engine finds every stream that crosses into the run area and computes its inflow hydrograph from the same rain applied over its catchment. The GLO-90 stream line and the mapped rivers do not always meet, so since 20 November 2026 each inflow enters at the nearest mapped channel cell within 600 m of its entry point (else the lowest cell next to it); before, 34 of 61 entries fell on land, and one built an 8.5 m deep pond on land at the southern edge. A design storm or typed hourly series is a point depth, while the mean over a large catchment in the same storm is smaller, so each catchment takes that depth times the areal reduction factor ARF = 1 − exp(−1.1 D^0.25) + exp(−1.1 D^0.25 − 0.0039 A), with D the storm duration in hours and A the catchment area in km², which is the single-equation fit of Leclerc and Schaake to the US Weather Bureau TP-29 curves in the form given by @allen2005; D is kept within 0.5–24 h and A at most 1,000 km², the range of those curves, so larger catchments keep the 1,000 km² value (0.82 for 6 h, 0.91 for 24 h). Until 17 November 2026 the full point depth fell on every catchment at once. Observed floods use the satellite rain of each catchment, which is already an areal mean, without this factor. Runoff depth follows the curve number method, the lag time follows the NRCS watershed lag equation [@nrcs2010] and the discharge follows the NRCS dimensionless unit hydrograph [@nrcs2007], integrated over 5-minute steps so that the runoff volume is kept exactly. Catchment area that lies inside the run area, or that was already counted at an upstream entry, is removed so that no rain is counted twice.

## 6. Rain and sea level

The design-storm option takes a rain total P and a duration D of 1 to 72 h. Within the storm, the depth of the most intense t hours follows the Mononobe intensity–duration relation used in Indonesian practice [@maitsa2021], P (t/D)^(1/3), and the hourly increments are ordered with the alternating block method [@chow1988], so the whole total falls with its peak in the middle; for D = 24 h this is the Mononobe distribution of a 24-hour total. Durations above 24 h extend the relation beyond the day for which it was derived, which is an assumption. Until November 2026 the option took a 24-hour total R24 and kept only the depth of its most intense D hours (94.5 mm of a 150 mm R24 for D = 6 h), which users read as the total. For scale, Halim Perdanakusuma recorded 377 mm on 1 January 2020 [@lubis2022]; users can also type hourly depths. The observed-flood option replays the three floods of section 8 with the hourly GPM IMERG rain of every 0.1° cell [@huffman2023]: each model cell and each upstream catchment takes the rain of its nearest cell centre, as in the validation runs.

Sea cells, defined as water pixels at or below 0.5 m connected to the open sea, are held at the chosen sea level throughout a run. Water reaching any other edge of the run area leaves through a boundary that passes the larger of the discharge arriving at the adjacent face and the edge cell's own Manning flow down the bed slope where the ground falls towards the edge (the outside is taken to continue that slope), capped at critical flow. Until 20 November 2026 only the arriving discharge left, so rain or river inflow on an edge cell itself was trapped (a test plane kept 0.209 m at its last cell instead of 0.047 m).

## 7. Display and export

The map shows depth per pixel by interpolating the water level bilinearly from the surrounding wet cells and subtracting the ground at that pixel, the water-level downscaling used for SFINCS results [@leijnse2021]. The ground used for drawing is the land surface: a river, canal or drain cell takes the mean level of the land around it, and this surface is interpolated bilinearly on every grid. Water inside a channel up to its banks is therefore the river itself and is not drawn as flood, water above the banks is drawn as one smooth sheet, and rivers do not show as a chain of 30 m squares; flood hazard maps usually mask permanent water the same way. This gives smooth water edges that follow the ground. Since 20 November 2026 a pixel is drawn only where its own model cell holds water above the land level, so the smoothing no longer spreads water onto cells the model keeps dry (before, about 35 km² of the 2020 map was drawn on such cells), and colours follow the depth classes 0.05, 0.15, 0.5, 1 and 2 m. Depth exports are GeoTIFF files in EPSG:32748, with the model depth per cell at 30–90 m and the drawn depth per fine cell at 10–1 m, together with a JSON record of all inputs, data versions, inflows and the water balance.

## 8. Verification

The WebGPU solver gives the same maximum depths as the CPU reference on real terrain to within 0.003 m, and both close the water balance to within 10⁻⁷ of the inflow. The scheme reproduces the analytical flood wave of @hunter2005 within 3 % of the inflow depth and matched SFINCS 2.4.0 on the same grid, rain and roughness with an RMSE of 0.06 m in maximum depth. These tests check the numerics and say nothing about agreement with real floods.

The engine was then run on the whole region at 90 m for three observed floods: 1 January 2020, 20 February 2021 and 4 March 2025. The 2020 flood was meant for calibration and the other two for validation, but choices such as pumps off, micro drains off and the start water of drains were later made while looking at all three floods, so all three scores are in-sample checks, not an independent validation [@klemes1986]. Rain came from the half-hourly GPM IMERG Final estimates on their 0.1° cells [@huffman2023], each upstream catchment receiving the rain of its own cell; the set-up is the app default (Jakarta Satu drains carved, terrain shifted by subsidence to the event date, channel roughness 0.035, average antecedent moisture, no pumps, no drain sink).

Current scores, 20 November 2026. After the review of six independent testers (verification/review-2026-11-18/), the engine was corrected at the run edges, river entries, 90 m river blocks and the map drawing (sections 1, 3, 5, 6, 7), and the three floods were run again (releases validation-jakarta-*-v33..v35, verification/observed/scores-*-imerg-v8.json, data/skill.json). The random baseline was also made fair: about a third of the reports lie outside DKI, so random points are now spread over all land of the grid and over land within 1 km of any report of the event (where people report), with 95 % intervals from a 1 km block bootstrap and a one-sided binomial test. The model hit 49 % of 260 reports in 2020, 39 % of 384 in 2021 and 39 % of 49 in 2025, against 45, 26 and 35 % for random points near reports and 43, 26 and 42 % over all land (intervals 41–58, 32–47 and 24–55 %). Only the 2021 flood is clearly better than chance (p < 0.001); 2020 is not (p = 0.11), and 49 reports are too few to judge 2025. Inside DKI alone the hit shares are 39, 29 and 18 % against 38, 21 and 37 % for random DKI land. At the reports the modelled depth has a median of 0.10, 0.06 and 0.07 m against 0.70, 0.65 and 0.55 m reported; the 2020 DKI flooded area is 73 km² against the 156 km² the city reported (whose definition is unknown). Over 2,610 RW, the critical success index is 0.13, 0.08 and 0.05, the false-alarm ratio at most 0.82, 0.85 and 0.94, and the area under the ROC curve 0.57, 0.59 and 0.53. Binary scores depend on how much land a model wets and on the threshold [@stephens2014], which is why they are reported against fair baselines.

Where the model misses (same runs, verification/observed/rescore_distance.py). Each report was placed in a class by the distance from its 90 m cell to the nearest river or water block of the model (channel flag; cell centres, so "within 100 m" means the eight neighbouring cells), and compared with random cells of the same class. Reports cluster along rivers: 43, 51 and 56 % of them lie in a river block or within 100 m of one, a zone that holds 29 % of the grid. In that zone the model is hardly better than random cells equally close to a river (in river blocks 53 vs 43 %, 30 vs 25 % and 31 vs 41 %; on land within 100 m 46 vs 43 %, 36 vs 25 % and 38 vs 42 %), and its median depth at those reports is 0.03–0.12 m against 0.60–0.85 m reported. More than 500 m from a river it hits 66 % (2020) and 64 % (2021) against 47 and 31 % at random (p = 0.01 and < 0.001; 4 reports in 2025). The model thus reproduces rain ponding on low land better than rivers spilling into the neighbourhoods along their banks. The cause has not been tested; candidates are smoothed satellite rain upstream, the size and depth of small rivers (section 3), and gates and embankments that the model does not contain.

Cell size (verification/observed/box_cellsize.py, cellsize-r04.json). All scores above are computed on 90 m cells, while a box in the app runs at 30 m. Five boxes of 4–5 km holding many reports (two for 2020, three for 2021; 177 reports at least 300 m inside the box) were run at 30, 60 and 90 m with the same rain, river inflow at the box edge and set-up. For 2021 the median model depth at the reports rises from 0.03–0.43 m at 90 m to 0.33–0.71 m at 30 m (reported 0.83–1.01 m) and the flooded land grows by 32–65 %; the share of reports whose own land cell is at least 0.10 m deep is 19–31 % at 30 m against 9–11 % for random points in the same box, and 2–24 % against 6–7 % at 90 m. For 2020 the depths stay at 0.08–0.27 m against 0.5–0.9 m reported at every size. A fixed 200 m search radius does not separate model from chance at 30 m (78–93 % of random points find a wet cell), so the same-cell rule is the fairer comparison across sizes. The 90 m scores therefore probably understate depth; the shallow 2020 result is not a cell-size effect. Five boxes are a small sample and box edges cut the floodplain, so this is an indication, not a region-wide score.

Sensitivity (releases validation-jakarta-*-v36..v38, verification/observed/sensitivity-r08.json). Each flood was run again with 13 variants: Manning n of every cell × 0.7 and × 1.3; the curve number of the built share of each cell 92 instead of 98; and ten terrain members with a smooth random error added to every active cell (0.5 m standard deviation, smoothed with a kernel of about 250 m; both values are assumptions of the order of the FABDEM error quoted above, not a measured error model). The unchanged run reproduces the scores above exactly. No variant changes a verdict (2021 better than random in all 14 runs, 2020 not better in all 14, 2025 too few reports). The report hit ranges 42–49 %, 32–39 % and 29–45 %; the median modelled depth at the reports stays 0.04–0.11 m against 0.55–0.70 m reported, so neither roughness, built-area infiltration nor a terrain error of this size explains the shallow bias. The terrain members move the DKI flooded area most (66–84, 22–47 and 57–78 km² against 73, 31 and 66 km²); Manning × 1.3 lowers the 2021 area to 20 km² and CN 92 lowers the three areas by 8–21 %. The weakness near rivers remains in every variant (2021: 25–35 % of near-river reports hit against 52–68 % of those more than 500 m away). The paragraphs below record the earlier, now outdated rounds.

Gauge rain (verification/observed/gauge_merge.py, gauge-rain-r03.json, releases validation-jakarta-*-v39..v41). IMERG smooths local storm peaks, so the three floods were run again with IMERG amounts adjusted to published daily gauge totals: the open West Java BMKG set of @saputra2026 (95–105 gauges in the rain box per flood, Bogor, Depok and Bekasi), the 28 gauges with coordinates in the January 2020 table of @jica2022 (BMKG, BBWS-CC, PJT2; Jakarta and Tangerang), and for 2021 the BMKG totals at Halim and Lebak Bulus. For each gauge the ratio of its 07:00–07:00 WIB total to IMERG over the same hours was spread by inverse distance with a pull towards no change beyond about 10 km, and the hourly IMERG rain was multiplied by it on a 0.02° grid, so IMERG keeps the timing and the gauges set the amount. Leaving one gauge out at a time, the adjusted rain predicts the missing gauge better than IMERG as is: mean absolute error 53.2 → 43.4 mm (2020, 120 gauges), 35.4 → 31.9 mm (2021) and 34.9 → 26.5 mm (2025); one factor for the whole area does not help. Effect on the flood scores (pumps off, drains 0): 2020 report hit 49 → 50 % (fair random near reports 45 → 46 %), depth at reports 0.10 → 0.10 m (reported 0.70 m), DKI flooded 72.9 → 76.1 km²; 2021 report hit 39 → 47 % (fair random near reports 26 → 32 %), depth at reports 0.06 → 0.10 m (reported 0.65 m), DKI flooded 30.7 → 39.7 km²; 2025 report hit 39 → 35 % (fair random near reports 35 → 33 %), depth at reports 0.07 → 0.05 m (reported 0.55 m), DKI flooded 66.4 → 56.3 km². Verdicts do not change (2021 better than random, 2020 not, 2025 too few reports). Better rain helps 2021 a little but leaves the model about 0.5 m too shallow at the reports, so rain is not the main cause; terrain, river depth and cell size remain (cell-size test above). Gauge-adjusted rain is a validation input only; the app keeps IMERG.

A citizen flood report from PetaBencana.id counts as hit when the model reaches 0.10 m on land, either in the cell holding the report or in the deepest land cell of the 3 × 3 window around it (about 270 m, since report locations are approximate). River channels and sea cells are excluded: a channel always holds water, and until November 2026 the score included them, so every report beside a river counted as hit. A score only means something next to what chance gives, so each rule is also applied to every land cell of DKI Jakarta: the share that passes is the hit rate of points placed at random. With the engine pack of 14 November 2026 and the window rule the model hit 50 % of 260 reports in 2020, 47 % of 384 in 2021 and 41 % of 49 in 2025, against 43, 28 and 43 % for random points; with the same-cell rule 23, 21 and 14 % against 19, 11 and 19 %. The model is therefore clearly better than chance in 2021, slightly better in 2020 and not better in 2025 (few reports). The old rule that counted channel water gave 72, 75 and 74 % for the same runs (`verification/observed/scores-*-imerg-v6.json`). At the reports the modelled maximum depth on land has a median of 0.10, 0.10 and 0.07 m, where citizens reported 0.70, 0.65 and 0.55 m. In 2020 the model flooded 83 km² of DKI land deeper than 0.15 m, against the 156 km² and 390 RW the city government reported. Place by place, over 2,610 RW polygons of the Sinking City dataset [@shabrina2026] (an RW counts as observed flooded when it holds a citizen report, and as modelled flooded when at least 10 % of its land is wetter than 0.15 m), the area under the ROC curve is 0.62 in 2020 and 2025 (0.5 = chance).

Those runs carried two processing errors found on 17 November 2026 (section 3): carved drains started filled up to the bank level the DEM saw, and permanent ponds counted as flooded land. Run with no rain and no upstream inflow over a 20 × 20 km box of central and east Jakarta at 90 m, the old engine pack flooded 14.6 km² of land deeper than 0.15 m (5.2 km² deeper than 0.5 m) from that start water alone, against 23 km² with the 2021 rain, while the corrected pack floods 0.1 km²; swapping the two changes one at a time showed that the drain start water, not the ponds, caused this. ## 9. Limitations

Tertiary drains, and micro drains outside South Jakarta, carry no size in the records available to the project, sluice gates are not modelled, and the NCICD sea walls are mapped without crest heights. The remaining city drains were represented as a sink on built-up cells, proportional to the built share, and calibrated on the 2020 flood: every capacity from 1 to 40 mm/h lowered the share of reports hit and pushed the flooded area below the reported one, so the app uses none (scored with the earlier rule that counted channel water; the pumps were re-scored with the land-only rule of section 8 and gave the same direction). IMERG averages rain over 0.1° cells: the cell of the Halim gauge received 166 mm over the whole three-day window, while the gauge recorded 377 mm in one day [@lubis2022], so the missing drains and the smoothed rain can offset each other and one event cannot separate them. Multiplying the IMERG rain by 1.5 and 2 as a sensitivity test brought the median depth at the 2020 reports to 0.62 and 0.80 m, but raised the flooded area in the five cities to 200 and 239 km², above the 156 km² reported; the factor that matched the report depths differed between events (about 2 in 2021, below 1.5 in 2025). The shortfall at report points is therefore not a uniform rain error, and the app keeps the rain as observed. Sea walls are not represented, so above a sea level of about 0.5 m results overstate flooding along the coast. The terrain is shifted by InSAR subsidence rates for 2016–2024 extrapolated at a constant rate back to 2013 and forward to the simulated date, inside DKI Jakarta only; earlier rates were higher in places [@abidin2011], and subsidence in Tangerang, Bekasi and Depok is not included. Channel cross-sections below the recorded water surface are missing.

In the design-storm and typed options, upstream rain is spatially uniform (the scenario depth times the areal reduction factor of section 5), and a lumped unit hydrograph for the largest catchment (the Cisadane, about 1,100 km²) is a simplification of its response. Agreement with observed floods was measured at report points and for the flooded area of one event; false alarms are counted per RW polygon (section 8), but an RW without a record is 'not recorded' rather than 'dry', so the false-alarm ratio is an upper bound.

## 10. Exposure

Buildings and people reached by a flood are counted on the 30 m model grid. Each building footprint of Overture Maps [@overture2026] counts once, in the cell holding its centroid. Each WorldPop 2025 count on its 100 m grid [@worldpop2025] is split evenly over the 30 m cells whose centres fall in that pixel, which keeps the totals. Kecamatan come from the Overture divisions (OpenStreetMap boundaries); Kota Bekasi and part of Depok have no kecamatan polygons there and are counted per city. A building or person counts as affected when the cell, or the coarser run cell holding it, is deeper than 0.15 m outside river channels, at the time shown or at the maximum. The counts say where people live, not who was present during the flood, and inherit the depth errors of section 8.
